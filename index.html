FROM THE CREATOR

About Fujítian and Fuju

¡Gřete! I’m Wyatt, and I’ve always been fascinated by linguistics and world-building. I’m a native English speaker and conversational in Spanish. I’ve had an imaginary world named Fuju since the age of three and to complement it, I’ve been building Fujítian, my constructed language, since I was six. It started as long lists of specific words scribbled down on graph paper with only some thought put into them, and I would teach my friends on the playground various early Fujítian slang. (“Crab poop” was among the most memorable.) As I got older, I reconnected with the old words and decided to make a coherent, and eventually complete, language. In freshman year of high school, I transferred my original word list into a spreadsheet and started making grammar rules. I was learning Spanish at the time, and had taken Latin fairly recently, and so many of the rules drew inspiration from Romance languages. The Fujítian words themselves were mostly based in Latin roots, with some homage to my original childhood ideas. I was particularly annoyed by the irregularity of Spanish conjugation, so I set out to make Fujítian a language sans irregularity that would be very easy to learn while being a unique and distinct tongue. One of the principal differences between Fujítian and English is its word order, which is a combination of most Romance languages and Japanese—not at all similar to English. As my dictionary and grammar rules grew, I moved over to a document which I’d add to over the next year until all grammatical bases were covered. The dictionary moved back to a separate sheet shortly after for ease of sorting, adding, and reworking. I recently created this website to present the hundreds of hours of work I’ve done on this language in a legible and graphically interesting way. It’s still a work in progress (especially features like the translator, which is more often wrong than not when fed complex sentences), but I’m proud of the product thus far. I recently added a unique Fujítian writing system developed from stylized pictograms based on and symbology relating to the type of sound and position in the mouth, with print and cursive variations. In that way, the script is most similar to Korean Hangul (although more related to Western scripts in its usage of letters). It is not yet included in the website, but I’m in the process of making the handwritten script into a usable digital font. I hope you find the project interesting. You can reach me at [TBA] for questions and/or feedback!

START HERE · 60-SECOND GRAMMAR OVERVIEW

Six ideas unlock most Fujítian sentences

No prior knowledge of grammatical terminology is required. Underlined terms provide brief definitions; subsequent topics give fuller explanations.

01Put the action last

A normal sentence places its first, then its , and finally its verb.

Diego Anna maðem'so possa.
Diego has his things for Anna (illustrative).
02Descriptions follow nouns

English: good things. Fujítian: maðem pom.

03Verbs show who acts

Endings indicate , , and tense. A subject pronoun is omitted whenever the conjugated verb identifies its person and number, except in grammatical constructions that require it.

04Small pronouns attach

Short can join a verb with apostrophes; recipients go before direct objects in the command pattern.

05Nouns and adjectives agree

Plural endings mark more than one. Adjectives also change to describe plural nouns.

06Accents preserve stress

The default stress is on the next-to-last syllable. Simple conjugations retain the original stress; present/past continuous conjugations stress the final vowel; future continuous uses -ĩ.

Subject

The person or thing doing the action, or being described.

Verb

A word expressing an action or state, such as hope, read, or be.

Direct object

What or whom an action directly affects. In “read a book,” the book is the direct object.

Indirect object / recipient

The person or thing receiving something: in “give Anna a book,” Anna is the recipient.

Noun

A word for a person, place, thing, or idea: dog, town, food.

Pronoun

A stand-in for a noun or noun phrase: I, she, they, it.

Adjective

A description of a noun: good in “good things.” Fujítian normally puts it after the noun.

Adverb

A word giving information about an action or description: quickly, often.

Determiner

A word introducing or specifying a noun: a, the, this, some.

Preposition

A word establishing a relationship such as place, time or direction: in, after, through.

Conjunction

A word connecting ideas or clauses, such as and, but, or because.

Clause

A unit containing a predicate (normally a verb) and its participants; a sentence can have multiple clauses.

Infinitive

The basic dictionary form of a verb, such as açet (“to do”).

Conjugation

Changing the verb form to identify person, number, tense or aspect.

Agreement

Changing one word to match a feature of another word, such as singular or plural.

Tense vs. aspect

Tense locates an action in time; aspect expresses how the action unfolds or is viewed.

Stress

The syllable pronounced with emphasis. Accents can mark where that emphasis falls.

Relative clause

A clause describing or identifying a noun, as in “the boy who gave Anna a book.”

Auxiliary verb

A helping verb that expresses tense, aspect, conditionality or possibility; English would and have are common examples.

Participial form

A verb-derived form used in larger constructions. The Fujítian perfect uses a verbal adjective ending in -ada, not the bare English past participle.

Finite verb

A conjugated verb marked for a subject and tense; compare English read versus reads.

Clitic

A short grammatical form that attaches to a neighboring word, often written with an apostrophe.

Aspect

How an event unfolds: simple, ongoing (continuous), or related to another time (perfect).

Recipient

The person receiving something in a sentence like “She gives her friend a book.”

Complement

A word or phrase needed to complete the meaning of another word or the whole clause.

Participle vs. conditional

In “would have talked,” English uses a participle, but Fujítian’s stated conditional rule uses a past-conjugated lexical verb followed by conditional açet.

CLAUSE WORD ORDER

Subject → complements → verb

Where English usually puts the finite verb immediately after the subject, Fujítian normally holds the action until after its objects or other complements. Determiners precede nouns, but adjectives follow them. Fujítian permits omitting a subject pronoun when conjugation already makes the actor clear.

DiegosubjectAnnaadditional participantmaðem'sohis things · noun + possessionpossahas · present, third-person singular
Diego Anna maðem'so possa.Diego has his things for Anna (illustrative word order).

How it is formed: maðem means “things”; 'so attaches a subject-linked possessive, producing maðem'so. possa is the third-person singular present form of possèt (“to have”), so it does not mean “gives.” The extra participant Anna precedes the object in this illustrative sequence; a precise recipient or benefactive interpretation requires the intended clause context.

WHEN THE CLAUSE HAS MORE THAN ONE VERB

Finite verb after the complement

In the Daniel passage, the walking action is expressed as an infinitive and appears before the decision verb. This is a more revealing example than a short subject–object–verb sentence.

Dánielactormópedètto walkða domemthrough townélegètadecided
Dániel mópedèt ða domem élegèta.Daniel decided to take a walk through town.

Adjectives follow their nouns

Fujítian preserves the conventional order of descriptions among adjectives but places them after the noun. So the sequence “small white dog” becomes a noun followed by size and color.

canin min plúselumismall white dog

Relative clauses follow their noun phrases

Unlike a final aside, a Fujítian relative clause sits immediately after its noun phrase. The Daniel story places qeho (“whom”) directly after the old friend, then places the negative perfect inside the relative clause.

ami ança qeho no sel’è occutada in annoman old friend whom he hadn't seen in years
Object order at a glance: subject → recipient → direct object → finite verb. In many ordinary clauses, the subject pronoun may be left out when the verb ending makes it clear.

PERFECT CONSTRUCTIONS

The auxiliary è and verbal adjectives

Perfect meanings do not use the ordinary finite endings alone. The construction includes a pronoun attached to è and a verbal adjective in -ada. The participial form itself distinguishes present, past, or future time; continuous perfect moves stress to the final vowel of the verbal adjective, without adding a separate vowel.

present perfectjaī’è áçadaI have done
past perfect progressiveoales’è açetadáwe had been doing
future perfectteles’è áçīadayou all will have done
no sel’è occutadahe hadn't seen (in years)
THE CONDITIONAL

Conditional auxiliary follows the main verb

The conditional auxiliary is formed from açet with a -q- element and follows the main verb. The language gives six person–number forms. In a past conditional (“would have spoken”), the lexical verb is past-conjugated, followed by the same six-person conditional açet form. Do not substitute the perfect è + -ada construction for a past conditional. Continuous conditionals use the same verb + auxiliary ordering provisionally; the manuscript does not supply explicit continuous conditional examples.

Infinitives and participles

The infinitive serves as the gerund form according to the examples. Separately, a present participle replaces the final t with u; a past participle adds -u after the past form, including its tense ending.

Article attachment and elision

e corresponds to “a/an”; le to “the.” Apostrophes bind articles to nouns. Before vowel-initial nouns, the article can elide or alter its vowel rather than remain a standalone word.

vowel-initial noun’anno / l’annoa year / the year

Possessor after possessed noun

In English, “Cameron's house” puts the owner before the noun. Fujítian writes the possessed noun first and connects the owner with o.

domin o’CámeronCameron's house
RELATIVE AND QUESTION WORDS

The u distinguishes asking from linking.

In a relative clause, the question-family word begins with qe-. To ask a direct question, an u is inserted after its opening q. This contrasts qeloc (“where” in a relative clause) with queloc (“where?”).

The dictionary also records derived nominal words, such as qeqisloc (“wherever”), by inserting -qis- after the relative prefix.

QUESTION AND RELATIVE FORMS

Relative versus interrogative wording

relative · what I wantLat es qema desri.That is what I want.
interrogative · when?¿Quechro sómniete?When did you go to bed?
interrogative · which?¿Quenum élegète?Which did you pick?

Direct questions carry opening and closing question marks. Relative expressions are embedded within other clauses.

Relative clauses in the Daniel passage

The Daniel sentence places the description of the friend immediately after ami ança. The clause includes qeho and the negative perfect no sel’è occutada.

ami ança qeho no sel’è occutada in annoman old friend whom he hadn't seen in years

Position of no in negated clauses

The language places no at the beginning of the clause it negates. In nested constructions, that can mean the negative particle occurs within the larger sentence rather than at its very beginning.

no sel’è occutadahad not seen
ADJECTIVE POSITION

Adjective order

Adjectives go after the nouns they modify. Within that post-nominal group, the language preserves a fixed descriptive order: opinion, size, age, shape, color, origin, material, purpose (after any determiner or quantity).

canin min plúselumismall white dog
contena min de’timbussmall wooden box

Adjectives are pluralized by changing their ending according to the language's adjective agreement rule.

ADVERBS AND COMPARATIVES

Adjective → manner, degree, quality

A base adjective can produce an adverb (via a -b- suffix), a comparative (-pl-), and an abstract noun (-s or -us). Accent placement retains the appropriate word stress.

strong · adjectivefortisfórtisbi = strongly · fort’plis = stronger · fórtisus = strength
quick · adjectiveveliçivelíçibi = quickly · veliçpli = quicker · veliçis = speed

Adverb position after the verb

English “quickly went” becomes a motion verb followed by its derived adverb. The Daniel translation puts velíçibi after mólèta.

mólèta velíçibiwent quickly

Adjunct nouns

The language describes noun modifiers built from a noun and a connective: de’ before a consonant and d’ before a vowel. The Daniel translation describes a wooden box with de’timbus—a noun-based material qualifier rather than a simple adjective.

e’contena min de’timbusa small wooden box

NUMERALS

Forming compound numbers

Multi-part numbers join descending-value segments with apostrophes. The grammar also defines prefixes for large values and usually marks an ordinal beyond the basic forms with final -i.

únegigrande únedecrande tríenonone billion, one thousand, thirty-nine

SOUND & WRITING

Apostrophe functions

The grammar uses apostrophes in several positions: between attached grammatical elements, when neighboring identical sounds contract, in articles and possessive phrases, and to separate certain consonant clusters. These are related spelling conventions but not all the same grammatical operation.

same-letter contractionle di’ássařotiduring the day I helped

PROSODY

Stress and accent marks

The default stress falls on the penultimate syllable. Acute accents mark exceptional stress and may preserve original stress after suffixation and attachment; the grammar gives vivís and dósidia as examples.

All ordinary vowels are independently pronounced, with ai, au, oa, ue among the one-syllable combinations (with ue ). The writing system distinguishes a single-tap r from a trilled ř, and sounds such as ç /s/ and ș /ʃ/.

Tapped r and trilled ř

r is a single tongue tap /ɾ/, like Spanish pero; ř is a rolling trill /r/, like perro. The distinction changes how a word sounds.

Vowel combinations

Most vowels are pronounced separately, but ai, au, oa, ue are treated as one-syllable combinations unless an accent marks a separation. The new ue combination counts as one syllable; its specific phonetic realization has not yet been defined. The language also distinguishes i from ì and ī; ĩ now marks future continuous.
