WHAT IS FUJÍTIAN?

Familiar roots, new rules.

Fujítian is a constructed language—a language deliberately created rather than evolving naturally through a speech community. Its vocabulary draws especially on Latin, while its grammatical design borrows and transforms ideas from Romance languages and English.

It was created to be systematic, prioritizing predictable patterns over long lists of exceptions. The reference grammar identifies vit (“to be”) as its single irregular verb.

03 / STRUCTURE & LOGIC

Inside the grammar.

Learn the rules that make Fujítian work. Explore sentence structure, conjugations, pronouns, and the details that distinguish it from English.

BASIC STRUCTURE

Put the verb last.

Unlike English's standard subject–verb–object order, Fujítian usually follows subject–complement–verb. Determiners precede nouns, but descriptive adjectives come after their nouns.

Diegosubject
→
cóma’osdirect object
→
ofraverb
→
Annaindirect object

Diego cóma’os ofra Anna. — “Diego gives Anna his food.”

IMPORTANT EXCEPTION

Recipients come after.

Direct objects come before the verb, but indirect objects—the recipient of an action—normally follow it. Subject pronouns may be omitted when the verb already identifies the subject.

EXAMPLE FROM THE GUIDE
Sol a’ófra’sal.

“He gives her it.” The direct-object pronoun a is attached before the verb; the indirect-object pronoun sal attaches after it.

Modifiers and clauses

Adjectives follow nouns

canin min plúselumi → small white dog

Adjectives retain their descriptive order but sit after the noun.

Adverbs follow verbs

mocúrpedèta velíçibi → ran quickly

Negation appears at the beginning of the clause; relative clauses follow the noun phrase they modify.

ARTICLES

Articles meet their nouns.

e means “a/an” and le means “the.” They join to nouns with an apostrophe, with vowel adjustments depending on the noun's start.

e + tomon → e’tomon (a man)
le + tomon → le’tomon (the man)
e + anno → ’anno (a year)
le + anno → l’anno (the year)

POSSESSION

Whose is it?

Possessive determiners include mi (my), ti (your), si (his/her/its), oali (our), teli (your plural), and seli (their). The possessive relationship is often expressed with the linker o and apostrophe.

domin o’mí

my house

domin o’Cámeron

Cameron's house

Diego cóma’so spectaca Anna.

Diego shows Anna his food. Here -so relates the food to the sentence's subject.

The grammar also distinguishes subject, direct-object, and indirect-object possessors with forms including -so, -do, and -io.

SEVEN QUESTION WORDS

The interrogative u.

Question words use an inserted u after q when asking a direct question. In relative clauses the word has no such u.

PUNCTUATION

Questions have two marks.

Just as in Spanish, direct questions use opening and closing question marks.

¿Quechro sómniete?

“When did you go to bed?”

¿Quenum élegète?

“Which did you pick?”

For nominal forms (“whatever,” “wherever,” etc.), the guide adds -qis- after the relative word's initial qe.

Conditionals

To express “would,” Fujítian places the conditional form of açet after the main verb, using -q- forms.

Habli açqi. → I would talk.
Hablatoal áçqoal. → We would have talked.
Conditional forms: açqi, açqe, açqa; áçqoal, áçqele, áçqala.

Imperatives

The command prefix ja’ combines with a second-person present-tense verb. Object pronouns can also attach.

ja’ofre → Give!
a’ja’ofre → Give it!
a’ja’ófre’jaī → Give it to me!

The guide also uses inverted exclamation marks for exclamations.

Adjectives & adverbs

Adjectives follow the noun. The guide forms adverbs with a -b- suffix, comparatives with -pl-, and nouns with -s / -us, adjusting vowels and accents as needed.

fortis → fórtisbi (strongly)
fortis → fort’plis (stronger)
fortis → fórtisus (strength)

Contractions & stress

Repeated adjacent sounds may contract using an apostrophe. Stress normally falls on the second-to-last syllable; an acute accent marks exceptions or preserved stress.

le dia + ássařoti → le di’ássařoti
vivís · dósidia

The guide also documents compound numbers, gerunds, and participles.

Two very different r's

r is a single tongue tap /ɾ/, like Spanish pero; ř is a rolling trill /r/, like perro. The distinction changes how a word sounds.

Vowel combinations

Most vowels are pronounced separately, but ai, au, oa are treated as one-syllable combinations unless an accent marks a separation. The guide also distinguishes i / ē from ì and ī.

07 / THE PROJECT

A language in progress.

Fujítian is an evolving constructed-language project. Its written reference describes a language developed over many years, drawing mainly from Latin vocabulary and the creator's interest in regular grammatical systems.

From an idea to a language

In the grammar guide's foreword, the creator describes starting the project as a young child, recording words over time, and later consolidating the material into a more formal and rule-based language.

This site is an introduction and interactive reference, not a substitute for the full grammar manuscript.

The written form shown here

The documents use Latin-based orthography with distinctive letters such as ç, ċ, ğ, ð, ř, ș, ì, ī. The broader Fujítian project may have its own developing script; this website shows the spellings supported by the attached grammar and dictionary rather than inventing those glyphs.
